V. Conclusion and Limitations

We studied the impact of artistic domain shift on feed-forward 3D reconstruction using MapAnything as a case study. To do so, we introduced a stylized multi-view benchmark derived from BlendedMVS, enabling controlled evaluation across multiple artistic styles while preserving ground-truth geometry and camera poses. Our experiments show that artistic stylization consistently degrades reconstruction quality, whereas grayscale and historical film degradations have little impact, indicating that the main challenge comes from structural style variation rather than low-level image corruption.

Among the evaluated adaptation strategies, feature normalization and AdaIN consistently degraded performance, suggesting that simple feature-statistic alignment destroys geometric information required for multi-view reasoning. LoRA-based adaptation achieved the best results, improving depth estimation robustness on stylized inputs while preserving performance on photographs. However, these gains did not consistently translate into improved point cloud quality, highlighting the difficulty of achieving globally coherent style-invariant reconstruction.

Our work is limited by the use of synthetic stylization, limited time and compute resources, and lightweight adaptation methods. Future work could explore larger-scale training, real historical artistic datasets, and stronger end-to-end adaptation strategies. It would also be valuable to identify which inputs were most out-of-domain using distributional distance metrics such as Maximum Mean Discrepancy (MMD), which could help diagnose whether the DINOv2 encoder or the MapAnything reconstruction layers are most hostile to multi-style inputs. Finally, it would be worth revisiting our deliberate choice to restrict adaptation to the DINOv2 encoder (Section III): once the encoder reliably preserves geometric signal under stylization, additionally applying LoRA to MapAnything's reconstruction layers could enable more targeted and expressive adaptation to stylistic variation than the encoder-only setting we study here.

A further limitation is that our study is deliberately scoped to geometry: we convert inputs to grayscale and do not attempt to recover color, so the question of color remains open. A natural next step is color recovery: rather than discarding color through grayscale conversion, one could learn to transfer stylized inputs into a photorealistic color distribution, yielding point clouds colored with faithful scene appearance and a direct measure of the color discrepancy between artistic and real imagery.
