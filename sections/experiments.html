IV. Experiments

Baseline evaluation

We evaluate the unmodified MapAnything on our curated benchmark. Because ground-truth (GT) point clouds are not available for our dataset, we derive them from the GT camera poses and depth maps.

Scale and pose ambiguity.
A fundamental challenge in evaluating 3D reconstruction is that predicted and GT point clouds are generally not registered: they may differ in global scale, position, and orientation. This holds even though MapAnything predicts a scaling factor. To address this, we apply Iterative Closest Point (ICP) registration, which minimises the distance between corresponding points in the two clouds. The figure below shows the steps of the algorithm.

ICP algorithm steps. Ground truth in red and predicted in blue. We see in the pre-aligned step that the point clouds are in a different scale and position.

Benchmark results.
After ICP alignment, F-scores are low across all conditions; results are reported in Table 1. Notably, the photograph baseline achieves a lower F-score (0.046) than every stylized variant. This is a counter-intuitive result given that MapAnything was trained on photographic imagery. One possible explanation is that the stylized inputs reduce high-frequency texture noise, producing smoother depth estimates that align more easily with the GT under the chosen threshold. We revisit this hypothesis below.

Controlling for alignment bias.
A potential confound in the per-style evaluation is that ICP may converge to a better local minimum for some styles than for others, artificially inflating their scores. To control for this, we cache the ICP transform estimated on the photograph reconstruction for each scene and apply the same transform to all stylized point clouds. However, this approach proved too rigid: because stylized reconstructions deviate geometrically from the photographic baseline, forcing a shared transform degrades alignment quality, resulting in approximately a ten-fold increase in Chamfer distance and a corresponding drop in F-score across all styles. We therefore discard this approach and allow each style to be independently registered.

Effect of stylization ratio.
Finally, we examine whether replacing an increasing proportion of input photographs with stylized images degrades metric depth prediction. As shown in the figure below, AbsRel increases monotonically with the fraction of stylized inputs, consistent with our initial hypothesis that distributional shift between training and test images is a primary source of performance degradation.

The results further suggest that even a small proportion of photographic inputs helps stabilize reconstruction quality, leading to lower reconstruction error compared to fully stylized input sets.

Destylization

We test whether the destylization baseline from the Method section — removing the artistic style before reconstruction — can close the stylization gap. We quantify each destylized output by its DINOv2 cosine similarity to the original photograph, a proxy for semantic proximity. To probe the pipeline fully, we jointly swept its two coupled knobs — the img2img denoising strength and the ControlNet conditioning scale — and compared each combination against a matched baseline: the stylized inputs on the same frames (heatmap below; scene_0, eight frames across the four styles). Every combination falls below that stylized baseline of 0.85. The best cell (strength 0.20, conditioning scale 1.30) reaches only 0.78 — still 0.07 below the untouched stylized input, and it gets there only by barely altering the image. Pushing strength up to actually remove the style collapses similarity (to 0.19 at strength 0.65), and raising the conditioning scale recovers little.

At low strength the painterly style survives almost untouched (nothing is really removed); by the strength needed to erase the style, the geometry has dissolved into noise. The heatmap below measures this same tradeoff quantitatively.

Destylization therefore not only fails to recover photographic similarity but actively reduces it at every point on the grid: it cannot remove the artistic style without moving the image further from the photograph. As a pre-processing step it cannot close the reconstruction gap, so we discard it as a baseline and focus exclusively on architectural adaptations that operate directly on stylized inputs. (Our destylizer uses a Stable Diffusion 1.5 prior; a stronger generator could yield more photo-like outputs, but those would be hallucinated detail rather than recovered geometry, so the conclusion holds.)

Robustness to Historical Image Degradation: Grayscale and Film

A key motivation of this project is reconstructing buildings from historical photographs, which constitute out-of-distribution (OOD) data relative to modern training sets. We compare the baseline model on standard RGB images against two degraded variants:

  • Grayscale — single-channel luminance representation replicated to three channels to satisfy the model's input requirements.
  • Simulated historical film — grayscale image with added Gaussian blur (radius = 1.0) and Gaussian noise (μ = 0, σ = 20) to mimic vintage lens softness and silver-halide film grain.

Contrary to our initial hypothesis, AbsRel remains statistically indistinguishable across all three variants for every artistic style, as evidenced by the heavily overlapping error bars. This reveals that MapAnything's DINOv2-based encoder relies primarily on geometric and structural cues rather than chromatic or low-level textural information, making it inherently robust to color loss, lens blur, and film grain, and therefore viable for direct deployment on historical archive imagery without domain adaptation. However, this robustness does not extend to artistic media (paintings, sketches, engravings), where the domain gap stems from structural style shifts rather than mere signal degradation, as addressed in the following sections.

Feature-Space Normalization Experiments

Our hypothesis was that stylized inputs induce covariate shift in DINOv2's intermediate activations, and that training-free Instance Normalization (IN) toward 𝒩(0, 1) would act as a style-invariant regularizer. We ran an ablation over two axes:

  • Number of normalized layers — from 1 to 24.
  • Cascading direction — front normalization (layers 0, 1, 2, …) vs. end normalization (layers 23, 22, 21, …), evaluated on 20 stylized scenes.

Normalization uniformly degrades performance across all styles, with AbsRel increasing monotonically with the number of normalized layers in both directions. The two strategies exhibit a notable crossover behaviour: end normalization achieves lower AbsRel at small layer counts (1–4), but its performance deteriorates steeply and overtakes front normalization around layers 8–12, after which it becomes the more harmful of the two.

This suggests that normalizing a small number of late-stage layers — where representations are more abstract and style-specific variance is lower — introduces only marginal disruption. However, as more late layers are normalized, the cumulative destruction of the high-level semantic structure feeding directly into the Alternating Attention (AA) block becomes severe. Front normalization, by contrast, degrades early spatial cues immediately but does so more uniformly, resulting in a slower degradation curve. In both cases, the core conclusion holds: training-free instance normalization cannot disentangle style statistics from geometric signal, directly motivating our learnable AdaIN approach.

Adaptive Instance Normalization (AdaIN)

To overcome the destructive effect of raw normalization, we evaluated trainable AdaIN layers optimized via cross-domain distillation: a frozen teacher model processes photographs while a student model (identical architecture with AdaIN layers inserted) processes matched stylized inputs, supervised by an L1 loss on predicted depth maps and 3D point clouds:

L  =  α   || Dstudent(Xstyle) − Dteacher(Xphoto) ||1  +  β   || Pstudent(Xstyle) − Pteacher(Xphoto) ||1

where 𝒟 and 𝒫 denote predicted depth maps and point clouds respectively, and α, β are scaling hyperparameters. All MapAnything weights remain frozen; only the AdaIN affine parameters are updated over one epoch on 800 paired image sets.

We ran the same ablation as above — varying the number of AdaIN layers (1–24) and cascading direction.

The outcome mirrors the IN experiments: AdaIN uniformly underperforms the unmodified baseline, end cascading is consistently less harmful than front at low layer counts before both converge, and more layers correlates with steeper degradation. This suggests the failure is not merely uncontrolled variance scaling but reflects a more fundamental limitation: lightweight affine transformations cannot bridge the complex domain gap between artistic and photographic inputs. While AdaIN shifts global style statistics, it likely disrupts the fine-grained spatial correlations within DINOv2's attention maps that are critical for multi-view geometric reasoning. This result directly justifies the move to LoRA, which operates within the attention projections themselves rather than on feature statistics.

Low-Rank Adapters (LoRA)

To evaluate whether parameter-efficient fine-tuning can teach MapAnything to be robust to artistic stylization, we trained LoRA adapters on a mixed-style dataset and evaluated across multiple configurations.

Implementation Details. LoRA adapters of rank r = 8 were injected into all attention projections (query, key, value, and output) of MapAnything's DINOv2 encoder, leaving all base weights frozen. An initial experiment trained with 2 views per sample (1 original photograph and 1 styled counterpart), but this configuration yielded a performance drop on 3 out of the 4 target metrics, likely due to insufficient style diversity per batch. We therefore downscaled the input image resolution to accommodate 4 views per training sample (2 original and 2 styled), providing a richer and more balanced mix of domains within each optimization step.

A key preprocessing decision was to convert all input images to grayscale before feeding them to the model. Since MapAnything directly assigns input pixel colors to the reconstructed 3D points without processing them through the learned architecture, stylized images would produce point clouds colored with artistic hues rather than true scene appearance, as seen in the Figure below. Rather than introducing an additional color-correction mechanism, we simply convert all inputs to grayscale, ensuring the reconstructed geometry is visually interpretable regardless of the input style.

Evaluation Protocol. We compare three models: the unmodified MapAnything baseline, LoRA without consistency regularization, and LoRA with consistency regularization (λ = 0.1). We evaluate them across three scenarios designed to probe distinct aspects of generalization: (i) 8 photographic views, to measure retention of the model's original capabilities; (ii) 8 impressionism-stylized views, to measure adaptation to heavy stylization; and (iii) a mixed scenario of 4 photographic and 4 stylized views drawn from different styles, to measure robustness in the realistic case where style is partially unknown.

Results and Analysis. The results (Table 3) reveal a clear split between depth estimation and point cloud quality. On depth metrics (AbsRel and RMSE), LoRA with consistency loss delivers the strongest depth improvements in most scenarios: it achieves the best AbsRel on both photographs and the mixed scenario, and reduces median AbsRel from 0.122 to 0.101 on impressionism, though it comes at a slight cost to RMSE in the mixed case. This suggests the adapter successfully learns to extract more reliable geometric signals from stylized inputs. On photographs specifically, the near-identical median AbsRel (0.084 vs. 0.080) confirms that fine-tuning on styled data does not degrade the model's original depth estimation capability.

Point cloud metrics (F-score and Chamfer), however, tell a different story. The unmodified baseline achieves the best F-score in all three scenarios, and LoRA without consistency obtains the best Chamfer distance in the impressionism case. Adding the consistency loss further erodes point cloud quality, suggesting that while the consistency objective effectively anchors per-pixel depth predictions, it does so at the cost of global point cloud structure.

This trade-off between depth accuracy and point cloud fidelity is further corroborated by qualitative inspection. The depth map comparison (Figure 5) favors LoRA with consistency loss, which produces the cleanest per-pixel depth estimates with the least edge and boundary artifacts. The point cloud comparison (Figure 6) tells the opposite story: the unmodified baseline struggles with stylized inputs, while plain LoRA yields the most visually coherent 3D reconstructions. Together, these observations highlight a fundamental tension — the consistency loss improves local, per-pixel depth accuracy but fails to preserve the global spatial coherence required for high-quality point cloud reconstruction.
