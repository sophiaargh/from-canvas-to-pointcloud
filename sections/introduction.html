I. Introduction

Three-dimensional reconstruction from images has advanced rapidly in recent years. Modern feed-forward models such as MapAnything [5] can directly infer camera poses, metric depth, and dense point clouds from sparse image collections without requiring expensive per-scene optimization. However, these models are trained almost exclusively on natural photographs and implicitly assume photorealistic image statistics. As a result, their geometric reasoning degrades under strong visual distribution shifts such as paintings, sketches, engravings, or watercolor renderings.

This limitation is particularly relevant for cultural heritage and historical reconstruction. Many destroyed, modified, or inaccessible buildings are documented only through artistic media rather than photographs. Examples include pre-photography architectural sketches, paintings of ancient cities, engraved archaeological records, and artistic reconstructions of monuments that no longer exist. Existing reconstruction pipelines cannot reliably leverage such sources because artistic styles alter texture, edges, shading, and local spatial statistics in ways that violate the assumptions learned during training.

In this work, we study artistic style shift as a robustness problem for feed-forward 3D reconstruction. Using MapAnything as a case study, we first quantify how reconstruction quality changes under controlled stylization of multi-view scenes. We introduce a benchmark built from BlendedMVS where each scene is rendered into multiple artistic domains while preserving the original geometry and camera poses. We then investigate whether style robustness can be recovered through lightweight encoder adaptations, including feature normalization, Adaptive Instance Normalization (AdaIN), and LoRA-based fine-tuning. We additionally evaluate a destylization pipeline that attempts to recover photorealistic inputs before reconstruction, as well as mixed-source scenarios combining photographs and artistic views within the same reconstruction set.

Our work addresses the following questions: (1) How severely does artistic style shift affect feed-forward geometric reconstruction? (2) Can lightweight encoder adaptations recover style-invariant geometric features? (3) Is destylization a viable alternative to direct model adaptation? and (4) How robust are reconstruction models when photographs and stylized images are mixed at inference time?
